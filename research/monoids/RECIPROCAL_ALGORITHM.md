# Effective atom divisors and atomic factorizations in the reciprocal family

This is a constructive extension of the proved reciprocal IDF/non-atomic
family. No novelty claim is made. It enumerates **atom-divisor classes**
and **atomic factorizations**, not all divisor classes: a fixed element
in this family can have infinitely many nonassociate non-atomic divisors.

## 1. Hypotheses and notation

Let a=ell^s, where ell is prime and s>=1. Let gcd(a,b)=1 and write

  b=product_{p in P} p^{e_p},  |P|>=2,
  a<p^{e_p} for every p in P.

Put t=a/b, S=N_0[t], T=N_0[t,t^{-1}], and

  D=product_{p in P} p^{e_p-1}=b/rad(b).

The hypotheses imply b>a^2 and C=(a-1)b/(b-a)<a. The following facts from
the family proof will be used:

* R=Z[1/b] intersect [C,infinity) is contained in S.
* The units of T are precisely t^Z.
* Every atom class of T has a representative

    beta=f(t),  f=sum_{i=0}^n c_i X^i,
    0<=c_i<a,  c_0,c_n>0.

For the last assertion, multiply a Laurent expression by a power of the
unit t to make its least exponent zero. A coefficient at least a forces
reducibility: use b-smooth h_L in S tending to 1, with h_L-1 in a^L R,
and C<a to divide the expression by h_L while keeping its other terms
fixed. Discreteness of t^Z ensures both factors are nonunits for a
sufficiently large index. Such h_L are obtained from the dense group
generated by suitable powers of two distinct primes dividing b.

All algorithms below use integer and rational arithmetic. Factoring
integers, enumerating their divisors, and polynomial division can be
performed by elementary terminating algorithms; no complexity bound or
practical efficiency claim is intended.

## 2. An exact cyclic-membership algorithm

For a positive rational y, the following decides whether y belongs S.

1. If a prime outside P divides the reduced denominator of y, return NO.
2. Let n be the least nonnegative integer such that y=N/b^n with N an
   integer. Equivalently, if the reduced denominator is d, take
   n=max_{p in P} ceil(v_p(d)/e_p).
3. Starting with y_0=y, for i=0,...,n-1 choose c_i in {0,...,a-1} with
   y_i-c_i in aR, and put y_{i+1}=(y_i-c_i)/t.
   More explicitly, if y_i=N_i/b^{n-i}, choose

     c_i = N_i*(b^{n-i})^{-1} modulo a,
     N_{i+1}=(N_i-c_i*b^{n-i})/a.

   The inverse modulo a exists because gcd(a,b)=1.
4. The terminal value c_n=y_n=N_n is an integer. Return YES exactly when
   c_n>=0, and in that case return the polynomial sum_{i=0}^n c_i X^i.
   Trailing zero coefficients may be removed.

The recurrence reduces the allowed denominator exponent by one, so it
terminates after n steps. The returned polynomial evaluates to y. For
necessity, if y_i=g(t) belongs S, its constant coefficient g_0 is
congruent to c_i modulo a. Write g_0=c_i+a*k with k>=0. Then

  (y_i-c_i)/t=b*k+sum_{j>=1} g_j*t^{j-1}

belongs S. Inductively every y_i is nonnegative. Thus a negative terminal
integer certifies nonmembership, and a nonnegative terminal integer
certifies membership.

This algorithm also works for y=0, returning the zero polynomial.

## 3. An exact reciprocal-membership algorithm

For a positive rational x, let K=0 when x<1. When x>=1, let K be the
largest nonnegative integer for which t^{-K}<=x. Compute it using exact
rational comparisons, successively multiplying by t^{-1}>1. Then

  x belongs T  if and only if  t^K*x belongs S.

The reverse implication follows because t^{-K} is a unit of T. For the
forward implication, every term of a nonnegative Laurent expression for
x is at most x. A term t^{-j} with j>0 therefore requires t^{-j}<=x,
so j<=K. Multiplication by t^K clears every negative exponent. When x<1,
no negative exponent can occur at all, which explains the choice K=0.

Apply section 2 to t^K*x. This gives an exact terminating membership test
for arbitrary positive rational x. It also gives a test for any proposed
divisor d of a fixed x: check d in T and x/d in T.

## 4. Bounded polynomials have unique Laurent expressions

If f in N_0[X] has all coefficients below a, its value f(t) has no other
nonnegative Laurent-polynomial expression.

First consider an ordinary competing polynomial g. By Gauss's lemma,

  g-f=(bX-a)h,  h in Z[X].

If h has a positive coefficient, let h_j be its first positive one.
Then h_{j-1}<=0 (with h_{-1}=0), and

  g_j-f_j=b*h_{j-1}-a*h_j<=-a,

contradicting g_j>=0 and f_j<a. Thus all coefficients of h are
nonpositive. If h is nonzero, its highest nonzero coefficient h_j is
negative, giving

  g_{j+1}-f_{j+1}=b*h_j<=-b,

again impossible because f_{j+1}<a<b. Hence g=f.

For a Laurent polynomial g, multiply both g and f by a sufficiently
large power X^m. The shifted f still has coefficients below a, so the
ordinary uniqueness statement applies. Cancelling X^m gives g=f formally.

Consequently, for f with all coefficients below a, f(0)>0, and f!=1,

  f(t) is an atom of T  iff  f is an atom of N_0[X] minus {0}.

Indeed a Laurent factorization of f(t) yields a formal Laurent
factorization of f by uniqueness. Shift each factor by its lowest power
of X. Since f(0)>0, the lowest exponents of the two factors sum to zero;
the shifted factors are ordinary nonnegative polynomials whose product
is f. A shifted factor equal to 1 corresponds exactly to a unit t^k.
Conversely, a nontrivial ordinary factorization gives nonunit factors in
T: a polynomial with positive constant coefficient can be a Laurent unit
only when it is the constant 1.

## 5. A finite formal-atom test

For a nonzero f in N_0[X] with f(0)>0, exclude f=1. To decide whether f
is a formal atom, enumerate all coefficient vectors for g satisfying

  1<=g_0<=f_0,  0<=g_i<=f_i for 1<=i<=deg(f).

Discard g=1 and g=f. For every remaining g, divide f by g exactly over
Q[X]. If the quotient h is a polynomial with nonnegative integer
coefficients and h!=1, declare f reducible. If no such g exists, declare
f an atom.

This test is complete: in any factorization f=g*h with both constant
terms positive, f_i>=g_i*h_0>=g_i and f_i>=h_i*g_0>=h_i. Thus every
possible nontrivial factor g appears in the finite enumeration. Integer
constant factorizations are included automatically.

## 6. Explicit bound for normalized atom divisors

Fix x in T minus {0}. Let X be the part of the reduced numerator of x
supported outside P union {ell}, and put

  L=ell^{s-1}*D*X.

For an atom representative beta=f(t) from section 1, set

  beta=N/b^n,
  N=sum_{i=0}^n c_i*a^i*b^{n-i}.

The last coefficient and the hypothesis a<p^{e_p} give

  v_p(N)=v_p(c_n)<e_p  for every p in P.

The first coefficient and a=ell^s give

  v_ell(N)=v_ell(c_0)<s.

If beta divides x in T, every prime outside P union {ell} has
nonnegative valuation on T. Therefore the outside-(ell*b) part of N
divides X, and

  N divides L.

Also c_0>=1 gives N>=b^n. Thus every atom-divisor class has a representative
among the explicit finite list

  {N/b^n : N|L,  0<=n<=floor(log_b N)}.

The logarithm here is only notation: compute its integer floor by exact
comparisons with successive powers of b. In particular the number of
atom-divisor classes is at most

  sum_{N|L} (1+floor(log_b N)).

Each class has exactly one representative with 0<=v_ell(beta)<s:
multiplication by t^k changes that valuation by s*k. The bounded-polynomial
representatives have this property, so the finite enumeration uses a
canonical choice of associate. Moreover N|L ensures v_p(N)<e_p for all
p|b; distinct enumerated pairs (N,n) cannot represent the same rational
number. The displayed bound is deliberately inclusive of candidates that
will later be rejected, including the unit 1.

## 7. Complete atom-divisor enumeration

For a positive rational input x:

1. Use section 3 to check x in T. If not, report that it is outside the
   monoid and stop.
2. Compute X,L and enumerate all pairs (N,n) of section 6.
3. For beta=N/b^n, apply the cyclic algorithm of section 2. Keep it only
   if the returned polynomial f has positive constant term, every
   coefficient below a, and f!=1.
4. Apply the formal-atom test of section 5 to f. Keep beta only if f is
   an atom.
5. Apply reciprocal membership to x/beta. Keep beta only if x/beta is
   in T.

The resulting finite list consists exactly of the atom-divisor classes
of x, with the canonical representatives specified in section 6. Every
step terminates, and completeness follows from the numerator bound and
the Laurent atom criterion.

## 8. Complete enumeration of atomic factorizations

Define the rational multiplicative invariant

  J(z)=z^s*t^{-v_ell(z)}.

For z in T, J(z)>=1, with equality exactly at the units. Indeed, if m
is the least exponent in a nonnegative Laurent expression for z, then
v_ell(z)>=s*m and z>=t^m>=t^{v_ell(z)/s}. Equality forces the expression
to be a single monomial of coefficient 1. Taking the s-th power proves
the assertion for J. In particular J(beta)>1 for every atom beta.

Let beta_1,...,beta_m be the complete list from section 7. For each i,
compute the largest integer B_i>=0 such that

  J(beta_i)^{B_i}<=J(x).

This requires only rational multiplication and comparison and terminates
because J(beta_i)>1. In every atomic factorization of x, the number of
factors from class beta_i is at most B_i, by multiplicativity of J and
the fact that every other factor has J-value at least 1.

Enumerate all vectors (e_1,...,e_m) with 0<=e_i<=B_i. For each vector put

  u=x/product_i beta_i^{e_i}.

Retain the vector exactly when u is a power of t. This unit test is exact:
write k=v_ell(u)/s; reject if k is not an integer, and otherwise check
u=t^k by rational arithmetic. Output

  x=t^k*product_i beta_i^{e_i}.

These are all atomic factorizations up to associates and ordering. No
output means that the nonunit x has no atomic factorization. A unit has
the empty factorization, recorded with its unit factor.

The final unit check is essential. Equality of J-values alone is not a
sufficient substitute for arbitrary rational residuals: for example,
when t=4/225, J(2/15)=1, but 2/15 is not an integer power of t and is not
a unit of T.

## 9. A sharper smooth-factorization corollary

Suppose additionally that

  ell^{s-1}<rad(b).

For an atom supported only on the primes dividing ell*b, X=1 and

  N<=ell^{s-1}*D<b.

Since N>=b^n, its normalized polynomial has degree zero. The normalized
atom is therefore an ordinary prime p<a dividing ell*b. Conversely
every such prime is an atom: the constant polynomial p has coefficient
below a and is formally irreducible, so section 4 applies.

Thus the atom classes supported on ell*b are exactly

  {ordinary primes p : p divides ell*b and p<a}.

Consequently a positive rational x supported on ell*b has an atomic
factorization in T if and only if it can be written

  x=t^k*product_{p|ell*b, p<a} p^{m_p},
  k in Z,  m_p nonnegative integers.

For prime a=ell (s=1), the extra inequality is automatic and ell is not
in the displayed prime set. The smooth atomic elements are exactly

  t^Z * <p : p divides b and p<a>.

As a concrete example, a=5 and b=144 satisfy the hypotheses. The only
smooth atom classes of N_0[5/144,144/5] are 2 and 3. Let t=5/144. Then

  243/128 = 1+4*t+630*t^2

belongs S and hence T, but it has no atomic factorization in T. Indeed
it is supported on 2,3 and has v_5=0. A factorization into the two smooth
atom classes would be t^k*2^u*3^v; the 5-adic valuation forces k=0,
leaving an integer, whereas 243/128 is not an integer.

In fact this element has no atom divisor at all. Any atom divisor must
be supported on 2,3,5, so its class is 2 or 3. But J(243/128)=243/128<2,
whereas J(2)=2 and J(3)=3. Multiplicativity and J>=1 for every T-cofactor
exclude both possibilities.

In fact 243/128 has no atom divisor in this reciprocal monoid. Any atom
divisor would be supported on 2,3,5, since outside valuations of the atom
and cofactor are nonnegative. Its class is therefore 2 or 3. But
J(243/128)=243/128<2, while J(2)=2 and J(3)=3, and every cofactor in T
has J-value at least 1. Multiplicativity makes either divisibility impossible.

## Reference implementation and checks

The exact algorithm is implemented in reciprocal_factorizations.py, using
Python's Fraction type and exhaustive formal polynomial factor checks.
For example:

    python research/monoids/reciprocal_factorizations.py 5 144 243/128
    python research/monoids/reciprocal_factorizations.py 5 144 5
    python research/monoids/reciprocal_factorizations.py 2 15 17/15

The first reports no atom-divisor classes and no atomic factorizations.
The second reports atom classes 2,3 and 5=(5/144)*2^4*3^2. The third
recognizes 17/15=1+2/15 as an atom.

Exact checks additionally covered 240 polynomial values and 1,200 Laurent
associates across (a,b)=(2,15),(4,225),(5,144), known formal factorizations,
and reconstruction of returned factorizations. These checks validate the
implementation on those cases; the proofs above establish termination and
correctness in general. The checks can be reproduced with:

    python research/monoids/check_reciprocal.py

A separate independent review also ran 1,103 exact checks, including unit
shifts and products of linear atoms, with no failures. Trial integer
factorization and exhaustive polynomial enumeration may be slow for large
inputs.
