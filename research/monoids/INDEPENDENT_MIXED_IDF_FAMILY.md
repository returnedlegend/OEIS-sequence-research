# A finite-basis theorem for independent mixed IDF/non-atomic pairs

This is a proved family inside the mixed, multiplicatively independent
region described in the supplied Open Problem2. Novelty relative to the
complete discussion and its unavailable PDF attachments is unverified.

## Main theorem

Let a,b,A,m,k be positive integers satisfying

    a>=2,  gcd(a*A,b)=1,  gcd(m,k)=1,
    P={p:p|b},  |P|>=2,
    a<p^(v_p(b)) for every p in P.                          (1)

Set

    q=a/b^m,   r=A/b^k,   S=N_0[q,r],   R=Z[1/b],
    C=(a-1)/(1-q),   D=b/rad(b).

Assume

    r>1,   r^m belongs N_0[q].                             (2)

Then S minus {0} is IDF and non-atomic, with trivial units. Every P-smooth
atom is an ordinary integer prime below a. Consequently every P-smooth
element of S in(1,2) has no atom divisor. If b is squarefree, there are no
P-smooth atoms at all; each integer p|b has no atom divisor.

The generators q,r are automatically multiplicatively independent. If the
least nonnegative residue of r modulo aR is greater than r, neither
generator is redundant. Such nonredundant examples occur explicitly for
every integer a>=3, as shown in section8.

## 1. Conductor, units, and smooth elements

From (1), b>a^2, so0<q<1 and C<a. The cyclic conductor gives

    R intersect [C,infinity) subset N_0[q] subset S.         (3)

Indeed represent an R-element using denominator b^(mL), choose its least
residue d in{0,...,a-1} modulo aR, and divide(x-d) by q. The allowed
 denominator depth drops by one and values at least C remain at least C.
This inductively proves (3).

Every P-smooth positive S-element is at least1. If x<1, a polynomial
expression for x cannot contain a term with q-degree0: such a term is
an integer multiple of r^j>=1. Every remaining term is divisible by q,
and hence has positive valuation at any prime ell|a. Since ell is not
in P and v_ell(r)>=0, x cannot be P-smooth.

A unit and its inverse are both P-smooth: outside-P valuations are
nonnegative throughout S and sum to zero on their product1. Both are
therefore at least1, so the only unit is1.

## 2. Smooth perturbations with enough congruences

For L>=1 let K_L=A^(m-1)*a^L, which is coprime to b. Choose distinct
p,s in P. The multiplicative group generated by p^(phi(K_L)) and
s^(phi(K_L)) is dense in the positive reals, and its members are congruent
to1 modulo K_L R. Choose h_L in that group satisfying

    1+C*q^L < h_L < 1+(C+1)*q^L.

Then (h_L-1)/q^L belongs R and exceeds C. By (3), this quotient is in
N_0[q], so h_L belongs S. These are P-smooth nonunits tending to1; pass
to a distinct subsequence as needed.

Suppose x in S has a representation

    x=F+a*q^i*r^j,
    F in S,  i>=0,  0<=j<m.

For L>=i, the congruence h_L-1 in A^(m-1)*a^L R implies

    z_L=(x/h_L-F)/(q^i*r^j) belongs R.

This follows because division by the monomial introduces only the
outside-R denominator a^i*A^j, which divides A^(m-1)*a^L. Also z_L tends
to a>C. The conductor eventually puts z_L in S, yielding

    x=h_L*(F+q^i*r^j*z_L).

Both factors are nonunits after omitting the possible single index where
h_L=x. Thus an atom cannot have a coefficient at least a in such an
expression. The extra congruence factor A^(m-1) must not be omitted.

## 3. Finite basis and the leading denominator term

By (2), choose a nonnegative polynomial F_0 with r^m=F_0(q). Repeatedly
replacing r^m gives every S-element an expression

    x=sum_(j=0)^(m-1) r^j*f_j(q),  f_j in N_0[X].           (4)

This is a finite positive reduction; no assertion of uniqueness is needed.
For an atom, section2 makes every coefficient of every f_j less than a.

The monomial q^i*r^j has denominator b^(mi+kj) and numerator a^i*A^j.
For0<=j<m, its denominator degree mi+kj determines(i,j) uniquely:
equality for two pairs and gcd(m,k)=1 force j=j' and then i=i'. Hence
there is exactly one highest-denominator monomial in (4).

If all the f_j have zero constant term, x factors out q. Therefore an
atom other than the possible atom q has a positive constant coefficient
in at least one f_j. The corresponding summand is a positive integer
multiple of r^j, which is at least1 because r>1.

## 4. IDF and a finite atom-divisor candidate bound

Fix x in S, and let X be the part of its reduced numerator supported
outside P. Let beta!=q be an atom divisor, represented as in (4). If n
is its largest denominator degree, write

    beta=N/b^n.

The leading monomial has coefficient d with1<=d<a and numerator
 d*a^i*A^j. At p in P, all of a,A are units and d<p^(v_p(b)); every other
term in the common numerator is divisible by b. Thus

    v_p(N)=v_p(d)<v_p(b).                                  (5)

The P-supported part of N divides D. The outside-P part divides X, since
the cofactor x/beta belongs R. Consequently

    N divides D*X.                                        (6)

Section3 supplies a summand at least1. When put over b^n, its numerator
is at least b^n. Therefore

    N>=b^n,   n<=floor(log_b N).                            (7)

Every atom divisor other than q belongs to the finite list

    {N/b^n : N|DX, 0<=n<=floor(log_b N)}.                   (8)

This proves IDF, with the inclusive bound

    1+sum_[N|DX](1+floor(log_b N))                         (9)

on the number of atom divisors of x. The1 allows for the possible atom q.
Units are trivial, so actual atoms and associate classes coincide.

As usual, the finite list includes candidates that may be outside S,
units, or reducible. This theorem does not claim a general irreducibility
oracle merely from a finite candidate bound.

## 5. Smooth atoms and atomless nonunits

A P-smooth atom cannot be q. Applying (5) to such an atom gives N<=D<b.
But (7) gives N>=b^n. Hence n=0. The atom is an integer, its coefficient
is below a, and ordinary integer factorization forces it to be a prime.
This proves the stated restriction on P-smooth atoms.

For h_L in(1,2), any atom divisor and its cofactor would both be P-smooth,
since valuations outside P are nonnegative on S and sum to zero on h_L.
Both would be at least1, while a P-smooth atom is an integer at least2.
This is impossible. Thus h_L has no atom divisor, proving non-atomicity.

If b is squarefree, (1) says every p|b exceeds a. No prime below a lies in
P, so there are no P-smooth atoms. Every nonunit P-smooth S-element is
therefore atomless, including every integer p|b.

## 6. Independence, nonredundancy, and effective primes

The generators are multiplicatively independent. If q^u*r^v=1 for integers
u,v, a denominator prime gives mu+kv=0. Hence(u,v)=(kz,-mz) for some integer
z. But0<q^k/r^m<1, so(q^k/r^m)^z=1 forces z=0.

Let d be the least nonnegative integer representative of r in R/aR. If
d>r, a polynomial in q representing r would have a constant coefficient
congruent to d modulo a, hence at least d>r, impossible. Thus r is not in
N_0[q]. Since q<1<r, q is not in N_0[r] either.

The effective denominator-prime set is exactly P: every p in P is already
effective for q, and no denominator outside P occurs. Every prime ell|a
is a numerator prime of q and has nonnegative valuation on r and positive
valuation on q, so it is not effective. Thus the nonredundant examples
lie in the stated mixed independent region of Open Problem2.

## 7. The power relation becomes automatic for large m

Fix a,b,A,k with r=A/b^k>1 and the arithmetic assumptions of (1), except
that m is allowed to vary. For all sufficiently large positive integers m
coprime to k,

    r^m>=a>C=(a-1)/(1-a/b^m).

Since r^m belongs R, the conductor (3) gives r^m in N_0[q], q=a/b^m.
Therefore the theorem applies for all such m. If the fixed residue d of
r modulo aR exceeds r, every member of this eventual family is genuinely
nonredundant. No unproved height restriction is needed in this corollary.

## 8. An explicit quadratic construction for every numerator a>=3

Choose any integer a>=3 and any squarefree b with at least two prime
divisors, all greater than a. Let s be the least positive residue of
 -2b modulo a. Because gcd(a,b)=1 and a>=3, this residue is nonzero, so

    1<=s<a.

Put

    A=b+s,   q=a/b^2,   r=A/b,
    K=(2*b*s+s^2)/a.

Since s=-2b modulo a, A=-b modulo a and A^2=b^2 modulo a. Thus K is a
positive integer and

    r^2=1+K*q.                                             (10)

Also gcd(A,b)=gcd(s,b)=1 because all prime divisors of b exceed s. The
main theorem applies with m=2,k=1. Since1<r<2 and r=-1 modulo aR, its
least residue is a-1>=2>r. Hence the pair is genuinely mixed, nonredundant,
and multiplicatively independent.

We conclude that for EVERY integer a>=3 this construction gives an
infinite family of mixed independent IDF/non-atomic examples. Each prime
p|b has no atom divisor in the resulting monoid. Infinitely many choices
of b are available simply by choosing two distinct primes greater than a.

Examples:

| a | b | q | r | positive relation | atomless integer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|3|35|3/1225|37/35|r^2=1+48q|5|
|4|35|4/1225|37/35|r^2=1+36q|5|
|5|77|5/5929|78/77|r^2=1+31q|7|
|6|77|6/5929|79/77|r^2=1+52q|7|

These are separate pairs from the unresolved examples(3/10,7/5) and
(6/35,19/3); the theorem does not assert their IDF status.

## 9. Two particularly explicit infinite exponent families

For r=37/35 and every even integer m>=2, set q=3/35^m. Then

    r^m=1+((37^m-35^m)/3)*q,

with a nonnegative integer coefficient. The theorem proves IDF and
non-atomicity for every such pair, and5 has no atom divisor. Nonredundancy
holds uniformly because r has least residue2 modulo3R and r<2.
The same conclusion holds for all odd m>=13, using the explicit relation

    r^m=2+((37^m-2*35^m)/3)*q.

Its coefficient is a nonnegative integer: odd m gives divisibility by3,
and the exact inequality(37/35)^13>2 supplies positivity for every such m.

For r=38/35 and every integer m>=2, set q=4/35^m. Then

    r^m=(38^m/4)*q.

Again the coefficient is an integer. Every pair is IDF and non-atomic,
with5 atomless. The least residue of r modulo4R is2>r, so these pairs
are also nonredundant.

## 10. An explicit numerator2 construction

The quadratic construction of section8 excludes a=2, but the general theorem
gives a separate family covering that numerator as well. Choose an odd b with
at least two distinct prime divisors, and an odd integer c satisfying

    1<=c<b,   gcd(c,b)=1.

Put

    q=2/b^2,   r=(b^3+2c)/b^3=1+2c/b^3.

Use m=2,k=3,A=b^3+2c. Then gcd(m,k)=1, gcd(2A,b)=1, and all prime-power
blocks of b exceed2. Moreover

    r^2=1+[c*(b^3+c)/2]*q^3,

where the displayed coefficient is a positive integer because b and c are
odd. Thus the finite-basis theorem applies: this monoid is IDF and non-atomic.
If b is squarefree, every prime dividing b has no atom divisor.

The generators are nonredundant. The residue of r modulo2R is1. If r belonged
N_0[q], its constant coefficient would be an odd positive integer. Since r<2,
it would be1, so (r-1)/q=c/b would belong N_0[q]. But c/b<1 has2-adic
valuation0; every member of N_0[q] below1 is divisible by q and therefore has
positive2-adic valuation. This is a contradiction. Also q<1<r rules out
q in N_0[r]. Multiplicative independence follows from section6.

The smallest convenient squarefree example is

    q=2/225,   r=3377/3375,   r^2=1+1688*q^3.

Its monoid is IDF and non-atomic, with both3 and5 having no atom divisor.
Together with section8 this supplies explicit genuinely mixed, independent,
nonredundant IDF/non-atomic families for every integer numerator a>=2.

## 11. A finite-state exact membership algorithm

The general finite-basis theorem also supplies a membership algorithm, even
when k>1. This section does not assert a general irreducibility algorithm.
Put H=k*(m-1) and let

    D={sum_(j=0)^(m-1) c_j*r^j: 0<=c_j<a}.

This is a finite set of elements of S. Accept0, all nonnegative integers, and
all elements of R at least C. Reject negative inputs and inputs outside R.
For each remaining state0<x<C, place an edge from x to

    y=(x-d)/q

for every d in D with d<=x and y in R. Membership of x is equivalent to the
existence of a path to an accepted state.

To prove completeness, start from any finite basis expression for x in S.
Its q-degree-zero coefficients are all less than a: their terms are at least
the coefficients themselves, since r>1, whereas x<C<a. They therefore form
a digit d in D. Subtracting d and dividing by q leaves an element of S and
reduces the q-degree of this chosen expression. Repeating reaches0 or an
accepted state. Soundness follows backward along any finite path from
x=d+qy, because d belongs S.

The graph searched is finite. Define the b-depth of z in R to be the least
h>=0 with b^h*z an integer. Digits have depth at most H. A transition from
a state of depth h has depth at most

    max(0,max(h,H)-m),

because division by q multiplies by b^m/a; if the result is in R, the
numerator factor a cancels without introducing denominator primes. Hence
all states below C have depth at most max(h_initial,H), and there are only
finitely many such rationals in[0,C). Breadth-first search with visited-state
tracking therefore decides membership exactly, including possible cycles.
The conductor test handles larger states without exploring them.

## 12. Bounded basis expressions are unique

Suppose two basis expressions have every coefficient in{0,...,a-1} and
represent the same rational. Subtract them and choose the largest denominator
weight n=mi+kj at which the coefficient difference d is nonzero. Weight
uniqueness from section3 makes this term unique. After multiplication by b^n,
all smaller-weight terms are divisible by b, whereas the leading term is

    d*a^i*A^j,   0<|d|<a.

For any p|b, the assumptions give |d|<p^(v_p(b)), and aA is a p-adic unit.
Thus the leading term is not divisible by p^(v_p(b)), a contradiction to the
sum being0. Therefore the two basis arrays are identical.

This uniqueness concerns bounded basis arrays. It does not say that every
bounded array has no alternative representation with larger coefficients.
The latter distinction is handled by the next section.

## 13. Detecting all excessive representations by a finite graph

Use the finite graph of section11, but explore ALL reachable states below C,
including integer states. Integer states must not stop the search: an integer
can have another representation whose tail exceeds C. A state above C is an
escape and need not be expanded further.

The equality case C in R is impossible here. Indeed b^m-a is coprime to b,
and C=(a-1)b^m/(b^m-a) could belong R only if b^m-a divided a-1. But
b^m-a>a-1 under the block hypotheses. Thus every state at least C actually
exceeds C.

If an escape occurs, x=F+q^L*y with F in S and y>C, so the perturbation
argument makes x reducible. Conversely, if ANY nonnegative basis expression
of x has a coefficient at least a, strip its successive q-degree-zero blocks.
Either an earlier tail already exceeds C, or the tail containing that
coefficient is at least a>C. Before the first escape all tails are below C,
so all their constant coefficients are less than a and are among the graph's
digits. Therefore that expression produces a reachable escape.

Call a member x escape-free when the complete finite search has no escape.
Every basis representation of such an x has coefficients less than a, and
section12 makes its basis representation unique. One can obtain this array
by following a successful path to a nonnegative integer; the final integer
is less than C<a. All atoms are escape-free, since escaping implies
reducibility. No closure under multiplication is asserted for this set.

## 14. A complete terminating atom oracle

Let x be a positive member other than1. If its graph escapes, it is not an
atom. Otherwise obtain its unique bounded basis array f and let h be its
largest q-degree.

Suppose x=yz with y,z in S. Express both factors in the nonnegative basis
r^j*q^i, 0<=j<m, and multiply. Reduce every occurrence of r^m using a fixed
positive polynomial relation r^m=F(q). The resulting expression represents
x, hence equals f and has every coefficient less than a.

Every coefficient of either factor is less than a: choose any nonzero term
from the other factor, and its product contributes at least the original
coefficient to some coefficient of the reduced product. Likewise no factor
can have q-degree greater than h. Multiplication adds nonnegative q-degrees,
and replacing r^m by F(q) never decreases a q-degree. All contributions are
nonnegative, so a higher degree could not disappear.

Consequently every divisor of an escape-free x has a basis representation
among the FINITE arrays

    sum_(i=0)^h sum_(j=0)^(m-1) c_ij*q^i*r^j,
    0<=c_ij<a.                                            (12)

Enumerate these arrays and test x/y in S with section11, excluding y=0,1,x.
A surviving proper factor proves reducibility; if none survives, x is an
atom. This supplies a complete terminating atom oracle for the whole family,
not just a finite atom-candidate bound. It also shows every escape-free
member has finitely many divisors and is atomic.

## 15. Complete atomic factorization algorithms

Filter the finite candidate list (8), together with q, using the atom oracle
and membership of the cofactor. This returns ALL atom divisors of any x.
The element q is an atom: any factorization q=yz has a factor below1, which
lies in qS because r>1, and cancellation forces the other factor to be1.
Every other atom is greater than1.

Fix a prime ell|a, set e=v_ell(a), and put E=floor(v_ell(x)/e). Every atomic
factorization of x contains q at most E times: valuations at ell are
nonnegative throughout S. After removing the q factors, the remaining
product is at most x*q^(-E). Among the finite atom-divisor set other than q,
let delta>1 be its smallest member when that set is nonempty. There are at
most floor(log_delta(x*q^(-E))) remaining factors. Exact rational comparisons
can replace logarithms in implementation. If x*q^(-E)<1, no factorization
exists. This gives a finite enumeration of ALL atomic factorizations,
including a decision that there are none.

The earlier warning after (9) applies to using a candidate bound by itself.
Sections12–15 provide the additional arguments that make the oracle and
factorization algorithms valid for this family.

## 16. Exact integer atomicity in the universal quadratic family

In section8's construction, the ordinary integer atoms are exactly

    {ell prime: ell<=a/2}.                                (13)

To see this, any integer atom is an ordinary prime ell<a: integers at least
a exceed C and are perturbably reducible, and a composite integer already
has an integer factorization.

Suppose ell<=a/2. Any first basis digit d=c_0+c_1*r that could appear in a
representation of ell satisfies d<=ell and

    c_0-c_1=ell modulo a,

because r=-1 modulo aR. As r>1, c_0+c_1<=ell. These inequalities force
c_0=ell,c_1=0. The only possible boundary alternative when2ell=a would be
c_0=0,c_1=ell, but then d=ell*r>ell. Thus the only first digit is ell and
the remaining tail is0. The integer ell has its unique constant basis
representation, and ordinary primality makes it an atom by section14.

Now suppose a/2<ell<a. Take d=(a-ell)*r, a permitted basis digit. Since
r=1+s/b with s<a, b>a^2, and2ell-a>=1,

    ell-d=(2ell-a)-(a-ell)*s/b > 1-a^2/b > 0.

The residue of d is ell modulo aR, so y=(ell-d)/q belongs R. Moreover

    y > b*(b-a^2)/a > a > C.

This is a reachable escape, proving that ell is reducible. This establishes
(13). These primes ell in(a/2,a) in fact have no atom divisor: candidate (8)
permits no noninteger atom with numerator dividing ell<b, while q cannot
divide ell<a and no integer atom divides the prime ell.

Every noninteger atom has strictly negative valuation at every p|b, since
b is squarefree and equation(5) gives v_p(N)=0. Integer atoms have valuation0
there. Therefore an atomic factorization of a positive integer can contain
only integer atoms. We obtain the exact description

    {positive integers admitting atomic factorizations}
      = {positive integers all of whose prime divisors are<=a/2}.             (14)

Their atomic factorizations are the usual unique integer prime factorizations.
For a=3 the set is just{1}; in particular2 is atomless. For a=4 or5 it is
{2^j:j>=0}, and for a=6 or7 it is{2^i*3^j:i,j>=0}.

For the squarefree numerator2 construction of section10, there are no integer
atoms at all (every integer atom would be a prime below2). The same valuation
argument shows that1 is its only integer admitting an atomic factorization.

## 17. Membership beyond the IDF theorem

The finite-state membership argument has a much broader scope. Let

    q=a/b in(0,1), gcd(a,b)=1, a>1,
    R=Z[1/b], r_1,...,r_t in R with every r_j>1.

No prime-block hypothesis or multiplicative independence is needed. The
cyclic conductor C=(a-1)/(1-q) gives R intersect[C,infinity) subset N_0[q].
For each j choose M_j with r_j^M_j>=C, so r_j^M_j belongs N_0[q]. Reducing
these positive power relations gives a finite positive basis

    B={product_j r_j^(e_j): 0<=e_j<M_j}.

Every basis element is at least1. At any unresolved state0<x<C, each
q-degree-zero basis coefficient is at most floor(C). Hence the possible
constant blocks form a finite digit set D. As before, place edges
x -> (x-d)/q whenever d<=x and the successor belongs R. Accept nonnegative
integers and states at least C.

If H bounds the b-depths of the digits, a transition from depth h has depth
at most max(0,max(h,H)-1). All unresolved states therefore lie in a finite
set with bounded real value and bounded denominator. Reachability of an
accepted state decides membership, with completeness following by stripping
any chosen finite basis expression. This proves exact decidability of
membership for every such finite mixed extension, independently of whether
atomicity or IDF is known.

This broad membership result does not inherit the atom oracle automatically:
sections12–15 additionally use distinct denominator weights and the strict
prime-block bound to obtain bounded-array uniqueness. General membership
methods are already part of the supplied discussion's cited general tools;
no novelty claim is made for this decidability corollary.
