# The pair (2/5, 2/3): exact atoms and an IDF reduction

Put q=2/5, r=2/3, S=N_0[q,r], R=Z[1/15], and h=5/3=1+r.
Then r=q(1+r), or r=q/(1-q).

**Provenance and scope.** Non-atomicity of this pair is already known in the
user-supplied November 9 discussion, which credits earlier work by
BakedPotatoz; the surrounding classification was supplied by Mardcelo.
The proof and formal-series reduction below were derived independently in
this session. Their novelty has not been checked. The IDF question is not
settled here.

## 1. A useful real conductor

Every x in R with x >= 5/3 belongs to S.

First, every z in Z[1/3] with z >= 3 belongs to N_0[r]. Indeed, choose
c in {0,1} having the same residue modulo 2 as z. Then
(z-c)/r belongs to Z[1/3], its denominator exponent at 3 drops by one
when positive, and (z-c)/r >= 3. Iterate until the denominator disappears.

Now suppose x>5/3. Starting with y_0=x, recursively choose c_j in {0,1}
so that y_j-c_j is in 2R and set y_(j+1)=(y_j-c_j)/q. Then y_j is in R,
and

    y_(j+1)-5/3 >= (5/2)(y_j-5/3).

At each step a negative 5-adic valuation increases by one. Once it is
nonnegative, it stays nonnegative. Thus after sufficiently many steps,
y_j belongs to Z[1/3] and is at least 3. It belongs to N_0[r], and the
recurrence reconstructs x as a polynomial in q and r. Equality is covered
by 5/3=1+r.

## 2. Units, small smooth elements, and coefficients at least 2

The units of S minus {0} are {1}. Every positive (3,5)-smooth element of S
is at least 1: if an S-element is below 1, its polynomial representation
has no constant term, so its 2-adic valuation is positive. A unit and its
inverse must both be (3,5)-smooth, hence both are at least 1.

There are (3,5)-smooth nonunits u_L tending to 1 from above, with
u_L-1 in 2^L R. For example, the subgroup generated by
3^phi(2^L) and 5^phi(2^L) is dense in the positive reals, and its elements
are congruent to 1 modulo 2^L R. Choose u_L in

    1+(5/3)q^L < u_L < 1+(8/3)q^L.

Then (u_L-1)/q^L is in R and exceeds 5/3, so the conductor shows u_L is in S.

If x has a polynomial representation containing a coefficient at least 2,
write x=f(q,r)+2m, where m=q^i r^j and f is nonnegative. For L>=i+j set

    z_L=(x/u_L-f(q,r))/m.

The congruence u_L-1 in 2^L R implies z_L is in R, and z_L tends to 2.
Thus z_L>5/3 eventually, and x/u_L=f(q,r)+m z_L is in S. Choose L also so
u_L is not x. Both factors of x=u_L(x/u_L) are nonunits. Consequently:

    An atom has no polynomial representation containing a coefficient >=2.

## 3. Formal series and uniqueness

Let T=N_0[X,X/(1-X)] inside Z[X,(1-X)^(-1)]. Substitution X=q maps T onto S.
Every element of T has a nonnegative integer formal power series, whose
coefficients grow at most polynomially. This follows by clearing a power
of 1-X.

If one of these series has a coefficient at least 2, that coefficient can
be exposed in a finite polynomial representation in X and X/(1-X).
Indeed, for any K,

    X/(1-X) = X+...+X^K + X^K * X/(1-X).

Expanding this positive identity with K sufficiently large realizes any
specified initial coefficient. Section 2 therefore implies that the
formal series of an atom has only coefficients 0 and 1. Since its
coefficients are eventually a polynomial in the index, a bounded such
series is either finite or eventually identically 1.

**Uniqueness lemma.** Suppose F is in T and all its formal coefficients
are 0 or 1. If G is in T and G(q)=F(q), then G=F as rational functions
and formal series.

Choose d so P=(1-X)^d(G-F) is in Z[X]. Since P(2/5)=0, the primitive
polynomial 5X-2 divides P in Z[X], by Gauss's lemma. Thus

    G-F=(5X-2)H,  H in Z[X,(1-X)^(-1)].

Write its formal coefficients as g_j, f_j, h_j, with h_(-1)=0. They obey

    g_j-f_j=5h_(j-1)-2h_j.

If h_j is the first positive coefficient, the right side is at most -2,
whereas g_j>=0 and f_j<=1. Hence every h_j is nonpositive. Put a_j=-h_j.
If a_j>=1, the next coefficient equation gives

    2a_(j+1) >= 5a_j-f_(j+1) >= 5a_j-1 >= 4a_j.

Any nonzero a_j would therefore force exponential growth. But H has a
denominator that is a power of 1-X, so its coefficients have at most
polynomial growth. Therefore H=0, proving uniqueness.

## 4. Exact atom classification

The atoms of S minus {0} are exactly:

1. q=2/5;
2. f(q), where f is a nonconstant 0/1 polynomial, f(0)=1, and f is
   irreducible in the multiplicative monoid N_0[X] minus {0};
3. F(q), where the formal series F has coefficients 0 or 1, constant
   coefficient 1, and is eventually identically 1.

Every series in item 3 belongs to T: it is a finite 0/1 prefix followed
by X^N/(1-X). In particular h=1/(1-q) is in item 3.

For the proof, Section 3 restricts every atom to a finite or eventually-1
binary series. A zero constant coefficient permits a factor X; the only
atomic exception is X itself. For a finite binary series, uniqueness makes
all factorizations formal polynomial factorizations, proving item 2.

For item 3, any factorization gives F=AB in T by uniqueness. Constant
coefficients of A and B are 1. Each coefficient of A and B is at most
the corresponding coefficient of F, so both are binary and are finite
or eventually 1. If both are eventually 1, their convolution has unbounded
coefficients, impossible. If one is finite and the other eventually 1,
the eventual coefficient of their product is the sum of the finite
factor's coefficients. This sum must be 1. Its constant coefficient is
also 1, so the finite factor is the unit 1. This proves irreducibility.

All atoms except q lie in (1,5/3].

## 5. Valuations of the atoms and finite endpoint divisors

For a finite binary atom f of degree n>=1, with coefficients c_0=c_n=1,

    f(q)=N/5^n,
    N=sum_(j=0)^n c_j 2^j 5^(n-j).

N is odd and coprime to 5. In particular v_5(f(q))=-n and v_3(f(q))>=0.

For an eventually-1 binary atom other than h, let i>=1 be the last zero
coefficient. Its canonical form is

    F(X)=sum_(j=0)^(i-1) c_j X^j + X^i * X/(1-X),  c_0=1.

Therefore

    F(q)=N/(3*5^i),
    N=3 sum_(j=0)^(i-1) c_j 2^j 5^(i-j) + 2^(i+1).

Here gcd(N,30)=1, v_3(F(q))=-1, v_5(F(q))=-i, and N>3*5^i.
The exceptional atom h=5/3 has v_5(h)=1. Thus:

    h is the only atom with positive 5-adic valuation.

Every fixed element x has only finitely many eventually-1 atom divisors.
If M_x is its numerator part supported outside {3,5}, the displayed N of
any such divisor other than h must divide M_x. There are finitely many N,
and N>3*5^i bounds i. The exceptional h contributes at most one atom.

In particular h is the only (3,5)-smooth eventually-1 atom: the other
endpoint atoms have a nontrivial numerator N coprime to 15.

## 6. An explicit witness of non-atomicity

Let x=125/81. Directly,

    1+r+r^2+r^3+5r^4 = 275/81,
    x = 1+q^2(1+r+r^2+r^3+5r^4).

Thus x is in S, and 1<x<5/3. If it were a product of atoms, every factor
would be (3,5)-smooth, since all valuations outside {3,5} are nonnegative
on S and sum to zero on x. Such nonunits exceed 1, so every atomic factor
would be less than 5/3. None could be h. All other atoms have nonpositive
5-adic valuation, contradicting v_5(x)=3. Hence x has no factorization
into atoms.

This independently recovers the known non-atomicity result for this pair.

## 7. An exact remaining IDF problem

For a positive integer M coprime to 30, consider finite binary polynomials
f with constant and leading coefficients 1 and degree n>=1 satisfying

    sum_(j=0)^n c_j 2^j 5^(n-j) = M*3^k,  k>=0.

Then S minus {0} is IDF if and only if, for every such M, this equation
has only finitely many binary polynomials f.

First one may impose formal irreducibility on f. The endpoint atom
divisors are already finite by Section 5. Every remaining atom divisor
of a fixed x is q or a finite binary atom whose odd numerator part outside
{3,5} divides M_x. (M_x itself can be even; only its odd divisors occur
for these finite binary atoms.) Conversely, if infinitely many finite binary atoms
have a fixed outside numerator part M, all divide the fixed element 3M:
indeed 3M/f(q) is in R and exceeds 9M/5>5/3, so the conductor applies.

The irreducibility restriction can also be removed. Every finite binary
polynomial with constant 1 factors into formally irreducible polynomials
in N_0[X], and all these factors are themselves binary with constant 1.
No nonconstant factor can occur twice: its square has a coefficient at
least 2, which a binary product cannot contain. If infinitely many binary
polynomials have outside numerator part M, but only finitely many possible
atomic factors with outside parts dividing M existed, their squarefree
products would give only finitely many polynomials. Thus infinitely many
such binary polynomials force infinitely many atom divisors of 3M.

This reduction leaves a variable-length restricted-digit Diophantine
problem. No finiteness theorem for it is proved here. Consequently the
IDF status of N_0[2/5,2/3] remains unresolved in this note, consistently
with the user's November 9 source.
