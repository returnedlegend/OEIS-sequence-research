# Atomic monoids with a real conductor and arbitrarily small atoms

This is an abstract counterexample, not a construction of a finitely generated rational semiring. It shows that the proposed atom-gap deduction cannot follow from the multiplicative-monoid hypotheses alone.

## Dense finite-rank group version

**Theorem.** Let Gamma be a dense finitely generated additive subgroup of the real numbers, and let c be any positive element of Gamma. There is an atomic additive monoid M contained in Gamma intersect [0,infinity) such that:

1. Gamma intersect [c,infinity) is contained in M;
2. the only additive unit is 0;
3. M has atoms tending to 0;
4. every element of Gamma intersect [c,2c) is a sum of two atoms.

In particular M has no positive atom gap despite its real conductor and finite-rank ambient group.

**Proof.** We build increasing finite atom sets A_n contained in (0,c). At every stage every member of A_n is an atom of the monoid generated by A_n. We use the following two extension operations.

### Operation 1: insert a tiny atom

Let A be a finite atom set and let eta>0. Choose epsilon in Gamma with 0<epsilon<min(eta,min A), with the obvious convention if A is empty. Such an epsilon cannot be a sum of old generators, so it will be an atom.

An old atom u could become reducible only through a relation

    u = k epsilon + s,

where k is a positive integer and s is an element of the old generated monoid with 0<=s<u. There are finitely many possibilities for s: all old generators are positive and their set is finite. For each positive g=u-s in Gamma, there are only finitely many k>=1 for which g/k belongs to Gamma. Indeed, after choosing a Z-basis of Gamma, every such k divides all the integer coordinates of g, and at least one coordinate is nonzero. Consequently only finitely many epsilon are forbidden. Density supplies an epsilon avoiding them. Thus all old atoms remain atoms and epsilon is a new atom.

### Operation 2: cover a prescribed conductor element

Let A be a finite atom set contained in (0,c), and let x belong to Gamma intersect [c,2c). Choose a in the nonempty open interval

    I = (max(x-c,x/3), min(c,2x/3)),

and put b=x-a. Then a,b belong to (0,c) and both exceed or equal x/3 (strict inequalities can be used). We choose a in Gamma and avoid finitely many forbidden values.

For an old atom u to become reducible, there would have to be a relation

    u = k a + l b + s = (k-l)a + l x + s,

where k,l are nonnegative integers, k+l>0, and s belongs to the old generated monoid. Since a,b>x/3>=c/3 and u<c, one has k+l<=2. Also s<c, so there are only finitely many possibilities for k,l,s. If k=l>=1, the right side is at least x>=c>u, which is impossible. Otherwise each relation forbids one value of a.

A reducibility relation for a itself must have the form a=l b+s: any occurrence of a on its own right side gives only the trivial representation. Here l<=2 and s<c, so again only finitely many values of a are forbidden. The same argument applies to b. Include coincidences with old generators and a=b among the finitely many forbidden values if desired. Density allows us to avoid all of them. Then A union {a,b} is an atom set, and x=a+b is covered.

Now enumerate the countable set Gamma intersect [c,2c), starting with c. Alternate operation 2 on the next enumerated element and operation 1 with eta=1/n. Let A be the union of the resulting finite sets and M the monoid generated by A. Every a in A remains an atom: any contrary factorization would use finitely many generators and thus already occur at a finite stage. Every element of M is by definition a finite sum of these atoms, so M is atomic. The inserted tiny atoms tend to 0.

All x in Gamma intersect [c,2c) belong to M, including c. For arbitrary y in Gamma with y>=c, choose k>=0 such that y-kc belongs to [c,2c). Then y is a sum of kc and a covered element, so y belongs to M. Positivity gives the assertion about units. This proves the theorem.

## Multiplicative P-unit formulation

For any finite prime set P containing at least two primes, take

    Gamma = sum_{p in P} Z log(p).

It is a dense finitely generated free abelian subgroup of R. Exponentiation gives an atomic monoid H of positive P-units satisfying H subset [1,infinity), containing every P-unit at least exp(c), with atoms tending to 1 from above. For c=log(min P), it contains every positive P-smooth integer. Its group of quotients is the full P-unit group: every g in Gamma is the difference of two elements at least c.

Thus even the exact finite-rank P-unit setting does not force an atom gap.

## Extension including every integer and the full localization conductor

There is a slightly stronger version. Fix any nonempty finite prime set P with at least two primes, put R=Z[1/product(P)], and put p=min(P). There is an atomic multiplicative submonoid S of R_{>0}, all of whose elements are at least 1, such that:

- N_{>0} is contained in S;
- every element of R at least p lies in S;
- S has P-smooth atoms tending to 1;
- its unit group is {1}.

Use logarithms and c=log p. Start with the finite atom set {log ell: ell is a prime less than p}. Enumerate log(R intersect [p,p^2)) instead of Gamma intersect [c,2c). In operation 2 choose a in the dense P-unit log subgroup; then b=x-a is the logarithm of an R-element, since division by a P-unit preserves R. The same finite-forbidden-values argument works for all old atoms. In operation 1 choose epsilon in the P-unit log subgroup. For each fixed nonzero rational logarithm g, g/k can belong to that subgroup for only finitely many positive integers k, by unique factorization of rational numbers (and for no k if g has a nonzero valuation outside P). So operation 1 still works.

Every R-element y>=p can be multiplied by an inverse power of p to land in [p,p^2), giving the full conductor. Integers below p are generated by the initial ordinary primes, and all larger integers follow from the conductor. The proof of atomicity is unchanged.

This S is constructed as a multiplicative monoid; no assertion of additive closure or finite semiring generation is made. Therefore it does not settle atomicity of N_0[5/6] minus {0}, but it definitively rules out proving that case non-atomic from the abstract conductor, integer-containment, finite P-unit rank, and near-one smooth factors alone.

## Near-one atoms may satisfy all the principal congruences

The tiny-atom operation can be restricted to any prescribed dense finite-index subgroup of the P-unit logarithm group; removing finitely many forbidden values still leaves choices in every nonempty open interval. Thus, for any integer a>1 coprime to product(P), the inserted atom h_k may additionally satisfy

    h_k - 1 in a^k R.

Indeed choose log(h_k) in phi(a^k) Gamma_P, whose exponentials are congruent to 1 modulo a^k R. One may also prescribe shrinking open intervals for h_k, for example

    1 + C t^k < h_k < 1 + (C+1)t^k,

with 0<t<1, C>0, and sufficiently large successive k. This matches the dense principal-congruence factors used in the cyclic-semiring arguments. The construction still preserves atomicity, so those congruences do not rescue the abstract atom-gap implication.

## Additive closure can also be imposed

When 2 belongs to P, take p=2 in the full-localization construction above.
Every nonzero element is at least1 and every R-element at least2 belongs to
S. Hence x+y belongs to S for every nonzero x,y in S. Adjoining0 therefore
produces a rational semiring whose multiplicative monoid is atomic and
non-IDF: its distinct P-smooth atoms tending to1 all divide4 eventually,
since4/h>2 lies in the conductor.

This semiring cannot be generated by finitely many rationals. Every positive
generator would be at least1; a semiring generated by finitely many such
rationals has finitely many elements in each bounded real interval, whereas
this one has atoms tending to1. Thus even additive closure together with a
real conductor does not settle the two-generator problem. Finite generation
and the presence of contracting elements remain substantial extra structure.
